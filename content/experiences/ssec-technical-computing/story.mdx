# Technical Computing at SSEC

The Space Science and Engineering Center at UW-Madison operates in the middle of real research, real infrastructure, and real scientific urgency. It supports work connected to major weather, atmospheric, and space-science efforts, including collaborations with organizations like NASA and NOAA. That made the role compelling from the start: the technical work was not isolated from impact. It directly supported the people and systems that keep research moving.

## Role Scope

At SSEC, my work blended systems administration, IT support, data-center operations, and day-to-day platform stewardship. A typical day could include reading ticket requests, helping users solve access or workstation issues, provisioning the right permissions, supporting lab and server workflows, or working directly on machine lifecycle tasks inside the data-center environment.

The role required switching contexts quickly without losing rigor. One moment I might be resolving a user access issue, and the next I might be handling machine changes, maintaining server inventory, or helping keep larger infrastructure workflows stable for the people depending on them.

## Platform Breadth

The environment was broad. I worked across Windows, Linux, and macOS, which meant the job was never just about one toolchain or one operating system. It required understanding the practical differences between platforms, supporting users where they were, and making sure that administrative work translated into systems that stayed usable and reliable.

## Scale and Impact

This was not a tiny lab setup. I helped support an environment spanning more than 2000 machines and servers, while resolving more than 500 ticket requests. That scale changed how I thought about routine technical work. Small mistakes compound quickly at that size, and good systems matter because they reduce friction for everyone else.

## Systems Work

Beyond day-to-day support, I built automation around software installs and updates so repetitive work became more dependable and easier to maintain. I also created a system for verifying and managing user-group relationships to servers and machines on `sgit`, which pushed the work beyond reactive support and into operational design.

Those kinds of improvements mattered because they reduced manual overhead while making access and machine management more consistent. The work was still grounded in service, but it also demanded system thinking: how to make the environment easier to trust, easier to scale, and easier to operate.

## Reflection

SSEC taught me that strong technical work is often quiet, but never small. Reliable infrastructure, clean access control, responsive support, and thoughtful automation create the conditions that let research teams move faster and with more confidence.

It also sharpened the way I think about engineering responsibility. Supporting mission-critical environments means balancing detail, speed, communication, and long-term maintainability. The work made me stronger not only as a systems-minded engineer, but as someone who understands how operations, people, and larger technical goals fit together.
